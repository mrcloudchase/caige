## What happened

Roberto Mata sued the airline Avianca in the US District Court for the Southern District of New York over an injury on a flight. On March 1, 2023, his lawyers filed an "Affirmation in Opposition" to Avianca's motion to dismiss. It cited several judicial decisions that did not exist, with fabricated quotations and internal citations. Avianca's lawyers told the court they could not locate a number of the cases. The court could not find them either and ordered the plaintiff's lawyers to produce them. They then submitted purported copies of the opinions, which were also fabricated.

The text came from ChatGPT. The sanctions opinion records that one of the lawyers had asked ChatGPT whether one of the cases was real, and that it replied the case did exist and could be found on Westlaw and LexisNexis. On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel imposed a $5,000 penalty on the two lawyers and their firm and ordered them to send letters to their client and to each judge falsely named as the author of a fake opinion. The opinion states that there is nothing inherently improper about using a reliable AI tool for assistance, but that existing rules give attorneys a gatekeeping role to ensure the accuracy of their filings, and it emphasized the lawyers' continued reliance on the fake cases after their existence was questioned ([Opinion and Order](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368/gov.uscourts.nysd.575368.54.0_1.pdf)).

## Failure analysis

**Category:** safety. The model produced false content without any adversary.

**Mechanism:** a general-purpose model, asked for legal authority, generated text in the form of legal authority: case names, reporters, page numbers, and quotations. Nothing in generation checks that a citation refers to a real document ([4.3](/training/module/safety-engineering/groundedness-and-hallucination)). Asking the same model to confirm its answer produced another confident generation, not a verification.

**Missing controls:**

- No retrieval from an authoritative case database; the model answered from its parameters.
- No deterministic check that each cited case exists in an authoritative source.
- Self-verification by the generating model, which is not evidence.
- The human check that the court expected (reading the cited cases) did not happen.

## Controls that would have helped

- Generate legal research answers only from retrieved documents in an authoritative database, and abstain when retrieval finds nothing ([4.3](/training/module/safety-engineering/groundedness-and-hallucination), patterns 1 and 4).
- Verify every citation deterministically: the identifier must resolve in the database, and any quotation must appear verbatim in the resolved document ([4.3](/training/module/safety-engineering/groundedness-and-hallucination), pattern 2).
- Treat legal output as high-stakes advice: require human review of every authority before filing, and present the tool's limits clearly ([4.5](/training/module/safety-engineering/sycophancy-and-vulnerable-users), high-stakes advice).

## Lessons

- Fabricated citations look exactly like real ones. Only a lookup against an authoritative source distinguishes them.
- A model's statement that its answer is correct is another output of the same model, not a check.
- Professional responsibility does not transfer to the tool. Systems built for professionals should make verification the default path, not an optional extra.
